You go nowhere in Beijing without your passport. Not into a museum, not into a park, not past a security checkpoint. And there are a lot of security checkpoints. Cameras track every corner. Reservations must be in the system before you arrive. Tiananmen Square has so many layers of security we didn't even bother entering. A glimpse of Mao from the perimeter was enough.

Giulia was here ten years ago and says it wasn't like this. Locals and expats confirm: the post-Covid security apparatus tightened everything. The city has the unmistakable feel of a place where order is not optional. It's not hostile. People are friendly, helpful, curious about foreigners. But you are always, always, in the system.

Preparing for this was its own ritual. VPN and eSIM to sidestep the Great Firewall, check. A battery of Chinese apps to pay, navigate, and communicate, check. Google, WhatsApp, and your credit cards don't work here. You enter a parallel digital ecosystem before you even board the plane. The border isn't at customs. It's in your phone settings.

---

The Forbidden City delivered. Breathtaking architecture, absurd scale, genuinely moving to walk through. The catch: 70,000 others thought the same. That's the daily maximum, and it feels like every single one of them is in your peripheral vision. During summer in Beijing, you fight the crowd with parasols and umbrellas. We acquired our personal weapons of choice early. A black one for me, a purple one with flowers for Giulia.

The real discovery was Ghost Street. Gui Jie comes alive at night: red lanterns strung across the road, smoke from a hundred grills, and the signature dish, spicy crayfish in a sauce that was genuinely insane. We ate at a place where they also had an ancient bento box on display as a cultural relic. The juxtaposition felt very Beijing. A city where the imperial and the street-level coexist without any sense of contradiction.

Half the time we had no clue what we were ordering. The food is incredible, and also weird, and also charming. Raw chicken in a hutong sounds like a recipe for salmonella. We're still alive.
